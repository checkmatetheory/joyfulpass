Knowing the Canadian citizenship test format up front makes it much easier to set a study target that means something. The test checks your knowledge of Canada and the rights and responsibilities of citizenship, and it's based on the official *Discover Canada* study guide.

## What's the pass mark for the Canadian citizenship test?

The written test is 20 multiple-choice and true-or-false questions. You get 30 minutes, and you need at least 15 correct to pass — a 75% pass mark.

IRCC sets the current format and eligibility rules, so confirm the details in [IRCC's citizenship test guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/canadian-citizenship/become-canadian-citizen/citizenship-test.html), especially if your notice to appear says anything different.

## What topics are on the test?

Questions can come from anywhere in *Discover Canada*, including:

- **Canadian history** and major events
- **Geography**, provinces, territories, and regions
- **Government and elections**, including how Parliament works
- **Rights, freedoms, and responsibilities** of citizens
- **National symbols, economy, and cultural life**

They don't cluster in one chapter. From what I saw, the applicants who came in confident on history but hadn't touched government or geography were the ones who got caught out — a good score needs broad coverage, not one strong subject.

## How to study for a passing score

Start with the current [Discover Canada guide](https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/ircc/migration/ircc/english/pdf/pub/discover.pdf), then use practice questions to find the gaps in your recall.

Try this:

1. Read one section of the guide at a time.
2. Answer practice questions on that topic with your notes closed.
3. Review the explanation for every wrong answer.
4. Take mixed, timed practice tests after every few sections.

Aim to clear 15 out of 20 comfortably across several different mocks. One passing score can be luck; a repeatable one means you actually know the material.

## Do you need to memorise every detail?

You need a solid grip on the facts, but memorising isolated lists is rarely the quickest way there. Tie the facts to the bigger story of Canada — how the provinces fit together, why the institutions exist, how rights are balanced by responsibilities. It sticks far better.
